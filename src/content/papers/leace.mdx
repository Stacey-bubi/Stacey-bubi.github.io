## The two conditions

LEACE asks for a transformation of the activations $X$ satisfying two things at once.

**Erasure.** The cross-covariance between the transformed activations and the concept
label $Z$ must be exactly zero:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(PX + b,\, Z) = 0
$$

Zero cross-covariance is what makes every linear classifier useless. A linear classifier
reads a linear relationship, and there is no longer one to read.

**Minimal damage.** Among all transformations meeting that constraint, take the one that
moves the activations least:

$$
\min \; \mathbb{E}\big[\lVert PX + b - X \rVert^2_M\big]
$$

The solution has a closed form. There is nothing to train and no adversarial game to play,
which is where the two-orders-of-magnitude speedup over RLACE comes from.

## Why whitening is the whole point

Here is the part that connects this paper to everything else on the list.

The obvious way to remove a direction is orthogonal projection: find the concept
direction, subtract its component along that axis, done. That is what
[abliteration](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717) does, and what INLP did before it.

Orthogonal projection assumes the space is round. It is not. Contextual representations
are [anisotropic](https://aclanthology.org/D19-1006/): the vectors occupy a narrow cone
instead of filling the space, and a few rogue dimensions carry variance far out of
proportion to the rest. In a space stretched like that, a step that is orthogonal in
coordinates is a large step along a high-variance axis in practice. That is where
collateral damage comes from.

LEACE handles it in four steps:

1. Take the covariance of the activations, $\Sigma_{XX}$.
2. **Whiten**: apply $W$, compressing the stretched cone into a sphere where variance is
   1 in every direction and features are uncorrelated.
3. Project *there*, in the well-behaved space, where orthogonality means what you want it
   to mean.
4. **Unwhiten**, returning the space to its original shape so the following layers do not
   receive a distribution they have never seen.

The projection is the easy part. Choosing the coordinate system to project in is the paper.

The 17-versus-360 rank comparison is this argument made concrete. INLP, projecting
orthogonally and iteratively, destroys 360 dimensions of the representation to erase one
concept. LEACE destroys 17.

## Rediscovery, and why one layer is never enough

Erase the concept at layer 5, probe at layer 12, and it is back.

The information is redundant enough across the residual stream that later computation
rebuilds it from correlated features that were left in place. So LEACE has to be applied
at every layer in sequence, each layer's output feeding the next, and only then is the
concept gone from the model rather than from one slice of it.

I find this more useful than the erasure guarantee itself. It says the linear direction
you found was one copy among several.

## What I want to do with it

The obvious experiment, which the paper does not run: use LEACE instead of orthogonal
projection to remove a refusal direction.

Abliteration's known cost is that the model gets worse at unrelated things — Qwen2.5-14B
loses 7.5 points of TruthfulQA. If that damage is geometric, caused by projecting in a
distorted space, whitening first should reduce it. Either it does, which says the damage
was an artefact of the metric, or it does not, which says refusal and capability are
genuinely entangled and no amount of careful projection separates them. That second answer
is roughly what [Safety Subspaces](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14185) argues, and it would
be the more interesting result.
