## Finding the direction

Run harmful and harmless instructions through the model. At layer $\ell$ and token
position $i$, average the residual stream activations over each set and subtract:

$$
r = \mu_{\text{harmful}} - \nu_{\text{harmless}}
$$

Normalise to unit length and you have a candidate $\hat{r}$. That is the entire
extraction method — difference-in-means, no training.

Doing this for every layer and every post-instruction position gives a large pool of
candidates, and the three selection scores pick one out of it. The layer cutoff
$\ell < 0.8L$ matters more than it looks: without it the search would happily return a
direction that just points at refusal *tokens* in the output vocabulary, rather than the
upstream representation that decides to emit them.

## Three things you can do with it

**Ablate it.** At every layer and position, subtract the component along the direction:

$$
x' = x - \hat{r}\hat{r}^\top x
$$

The model now answers harmful requests, and stays coherent while doing it.

**Add it.** Add $r$ into activations at one layer and a harmless prompt starts getting
refused. This is the half that makes the claim causal. The direction does not merely
correlate with refusal, it drives it in both directions.

**Put it in the weights.** Every matrix $W_{\text{out}}$ that writes into the residual
stream gets orthogonalised against the direction:

$$
W'_{\text{out}} = W_{\text{out}} - \hat{r}\hat{r}^\top W_{\text{out}}
$$

Appendix E proves this is exactly equivalent to ablating at inference time. Geometrically
you take the whole activation space and flatten it along one axis, so the model becomes
physically unable to write into the refusal subspace. What you get out is a normal set of
weights you can save, upload and share.

The cost profile is the point. Fine-tuning a model to remove safety needs harmful
instructions *and* harmful answers, plus an optimiser. This needs 128 harmful
instructions, one forward pass over each, and a matrix subtraction.

## What adversarial suffixes were doing all along

Section 5 measures how strongly activations point along $\hat{r}$ under three conditions:
a plain harmful prompt, a harmful prompt with a random suffix, and a harmful prompt with
an adversarial suffix. Under the adversarial suffix the alignment collapses to the level
of an ordinary harmless prompt.

Then they find the mechanism. Direct feature attribution identifies the attention heads
that write most strongly toward refusal. Normally those heads attend to the harmful words
in the instruction. The adversarial suffix pulls their attention onto itself. The heads
stop seeing the dangerous content, never send the signal, and generation proceeds as if
the request were ordinary.

So GCG is not finding some exotic new failure. It is suppressing this direction from
outside the model.

## Where I think it is weak

The extraction method is the obvious gap, and the paper is honest that its goal was to
demonstrate fragility rather than to find the best direction.
[Concept Cones](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17420) replaces the sweep with gradient descent
and finds not just a better direction but a whole cone of them.

The deeper problem is the projection. Orthogonal projection assumes the space is round —
that cutting along one axis leaves everything else alone. Representation space in a
transformer is [anisotropic](https://aclanthology.org/D19-1006/): activations occupy a
narrow cone rather than spreading out, and a few dimensions carry variance far out of
proportion to the rest. An orthogonal step in a space shaped like that is a large step
along whatever high-variance axis happens to be nearby. The TruthfulQA drop is what that
looks like from outside.

This is the thread running through most of the rest of the list.
[LEACE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03819) whitens the space before projecting for exactly
this reason. The [causal inner product](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03658) is an attempt to
define the right ruler formally. And
[Safety Subspaces](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14185) argues the target may not be
separable at all.
