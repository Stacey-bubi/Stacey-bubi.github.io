## Two measurements

**Anisotropy** here is a spectral quantity. Centre the embedding matrix, take its singular
values, and compute

$$
\text{anisotropy}(X) = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sum_i \sigma_i^2}
$$

the fraction of total variance carried by the single largest direction. Near zero means variance
is spread evenly, which is what an isotropic space looks like. Large means one direction
dominates. They cross-check it against average pairwise cosine similarity.

**Intrinsic dimension** asks how many dimensions the data actually occupies, regardless of how
many it is stored in. The estimator works from how sphere volume scales: for each point, take
the distances to its first and second nearest neighbours and fit the distribution of their
ratio. Two further estimators are used to verify.

The models are a wide sweep — BERT, RoBERTa and ALBERT on the encoder side; OPT, Llama-2, GPT-2,
GPT-J, Falcon, Bloom, Pythia and TinyLlama on the decoder side.

## Where the distortion lives

The result I use is the encoder/decoder split.

Encoders are fairly evenly anisotropic through their depth. Decoders are not: their anisotropy
profile is bell-shaped, peaking in the middle layers.

That is worth putting next to how refusal directions are extracted.
[Arditi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717) sweep layers and exclude the last 20% of the
network, so the chosen direction typically comes from the middle. According to this paper, the
middle is the most geometrically distorted part of a decoder.

So the standard abliteration recipe picks its direction from exactly the region where orthogonal
projection is least justified. That does not make the attack wrong — it plainly works — but it is
a plausible source of the collateral damage, and it suggests the
[whitening correction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03819) would matter most precisely where these
methods operate.

This is also the paper that makes anisotropy actionable rather than merely true.
[Ethayarajh](https://aclanthology.org/D19-1006/) established the phenomenon in 2019 on small
models; this says where it is worst in the decoders people actually intervene on today. And
$\sigma_1^2 / \sum \sigma_i^2$ is cheap enough that there is no excuse for not reporting it
alongside an intervention result. If you are projecting in a space, say how distorted the space
was.

## The training dynamics

Anisotropy in decoders grows through training and then levels off. The narrow cone is something
the model builds, not something it starts with.

Intrinsic dimension moves in two phases: up first, as the model spreads information into more
dimensions, then down, as it compresses into more compact representations. The final model is not
the most dimensionally spread version of itself — it passed through that on the way. A nice echo
of the information-bottleneck picture, arrived at from geometry rather than information theory.

## Its limits

It is descriptive. It measures the geometry and does not connect it to any downstream task or
intervention outcome, so the practical cost of the distortion is still unquantified anywhere in
this literature.

Intrinsic dimension estimators also disagree with each other in high dimensions and on limited
samples; using three of them helps, but the absolute values should be read as indicative rather
than exact. And defining anisotropy as the first singular value's share captures a single
dominant direction well while saying less when several rogue dimensions share the load.
