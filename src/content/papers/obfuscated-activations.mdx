## The threat model

Latent-space defenses work on a reasonable premise. Even if you cannot filter every harmful
output, harmful behaviour must pass through the model's internal states, so watch those
instead. Train a probe on activations, or use a sparse autoencoder, or an OOD detector, and
catch the behaviour before it surfaces.

This paper asks whether the internal state is a reliable place to watch, and finds it is
not. Activations are malleable: the same harmful output can be produced from many different
internal configurations, and an attacker can choose one the monitor was not trained to
flag.

## Four ways in

The attacks are graded by how much access the attacker has, which is what makes the result
hard to dismiss:

- **Embedding suffix attacks.** A universal suffix in embedding space, optimised jointly
  for the harmful behaviour and for evading the monitor. White-box.
- **Hard prompt attacks.** Real tokens, optimised per input with a gradient-free method.
  Gray-box — output access only.
- **Fine-tuning with an obfuscation loss.** When the attacker controls training, evasion
  becomes an explicit training objective.
- **Data poisoning.** Choose trigger complexity and poison rate carefully and obfuscation
  appears without anyone optimising for it.

That last one is the most uncomfortable. Evasion does not require an adversary who
understands the defence. It can be a side effect of how the poisoned data was constructed.

## The numbers that matter

Probe recall goes from 100% to 0% while about 90% of jailbreaks still succeed. The monitor
does not degrade — it stops working, while the attack keeps working.

Generation-based probes, which look at activations during generation rather than on the
prompt, do better against ordinary jailbreaks: over 90% recall at a 1% false positive rate.
Against obfuscation they fail completely.

And sparse autoencoders offer no extra protection. This one is worth dwelling on, because
SAEs are frequently described as recovering the features the model actually uses, which
should make an SAE-based monitor more principled than a probe fitted to a label. Under
attack they behave the same.

## Where the hope is

Obfuscation is not free. On SQL generation, obfuscating against more layers correlates
significantly with worse performance. Constraining your own internal states enough to fool
a monitor costs you the ability to do things that need those states.

That points somewhere different from better detectors. If evasion is expensive on hard
tasks, a defence might be built on requiring the model to do something that genuinely needs
its full representational capacity — making obfuscation and capability compete — rather than
on recognising what harm looks like from the inside.
