It happened historically: first came an architecture that turns text into text, and is useful exactly for that, and only afterwards came the constraints and the mechanisms meant to keep users and everyone else safe.
There are a lot of those machinery. Some sit outside the weights, in the harness: input/output classifiers like [Llama Guard](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06674) or [Constitutional Classifiers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.18837); system prompts that state the rules at runtime; monitoring, meaning offline log review and chain-of-thought inspection; access controls — account checks, rate limits, bans, trusted-user tiers; agent controls like sandboxing, tool permissions, human approval for risky actions; and weight security, which stops someone stealing the weights and fine-tuning the safeguards off.
Others change the weights. Pretraining data filtering removes dangerous content before the model ever learns it. SFT on safe answers teaches refusals by example. Preference training ([RLHF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155), [RLAIF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00267), [Constitutional AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073), [DPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)) rewards safe and helpful answers over harmful ones. [Instruction hierarchy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13208) teaches the model to rank system prompt above user above tool output. Adversarial training trains on jailbreaks — the [latent variant](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05030) (Casper et al., 2024) attacks activations instead of inputs. [Unlearning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03218) (RMU, Li et al., 2024) tries to cut out specific dangerous knowledge. [Circuit breakers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04313) (Zou et al., 2024) train harmful internal states to get redirected so the model can't continue. Abstention training teaches saying "I don't know". [Deliberative alignment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.16339) (Guan et al., 2024) trains the model to reason over a written safety spec before it answers.
There are more. But the most visible one is refusal. You ask for something harmful, the model says *"I'm sorry, I can't help with that"*.


I spent a long time running evals on models in medical domains, so whether I wanted to or not I kept hitting refusals of every kind. Back then I only worked with behavioural artifacts, not internals, and the mechanism already bothered me. At what stage was it learned, and at what stage should it actually be introduced? How reliable is it? Can you flip the model's decision with interventions in weights or activations — make it refuse safe requests and answer dangerous ones? How far does that generalise across models that differ in size, architecture, corpus, alignment? Are open-weight models in trouble? Some of these got answered in the papers below. Some are naive. Some are philosophical.

The paper that opened the door to interpretability for me, and indirectly to AI safety, is a bit of a double-edged sword: [Refusal in Language Models Is Mediated by a Single Direction, Arditi et al. (2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717).
TLDR: across 13 open models, authors found that refusal is mediated by **one direction** in the residual stream. Erasing that direction caused the AI to stop refusing requests.. Add it and the model refuses to explain yoga — mildly irritating overrefusal. Small price to save the day, I'd say.
But if one direction and a few lines of hook code switch off refusal across a whole model family, then "the model refuses" is not something you can lean on for an open-weight model. That's worth measuring rather than assuming, because it feeds into what gets released and which safety arguments get taken seriously.

---

## Why a dose-response curve

Arditi remove the whole direction. That answers *can it be done*, not *how cheaply*.

Look at it from the attacker's side. Full ablation is cheap to run, but it's blunt: you've flattened one axis everywhere in the network and you don't know what else was riding on it. What an attacker actually wants is the smallest edit that kills refusal and leaves the model useful. So — is there a cheap setting? Does the guardrail degrade gracefully and give you a dial, or does it snap? And does pushing harder cost you more of the model's general capabilities?
The experiment: I took the family Arditi didn't test (Qwen2.5-Instruct), put a scaling factor `α` on the ablation, and swept it from 0 to 1.25.

---

## Finding the direction, removing it

**Residual stream.** A decoder transformer keeps a running vector per token, and every layer adds into it:

```
x̃⁽ˡ⁾ = x⁽ˡ⁾ + Attn⁽ˡ⁾(x⁽ˡ⁾)
x⁽ˡ⁺¹⁾ = x̃⁽ˡ⁾ + MLP⁽ˡ⁾(x̃⁽ˡ⁾)
```

Everything writes into the same vector, so you can treat it as a shared workspace where different features sit in different directions. That's the linear representation hypothesis, and the whole method rests on it.

**Difference-in-means.** Run the model on harmful prompts, run it on harmless prompts, average the activations, subtract:

```
r⁽ˡ⁾ᵢ = mean(harmful) − mean(harmless)    at layer l, position i
```

`r` is the candidate refusal direction, `r̂` its unit version. Crude, but it works, and there's theory saying diff-in-means is a reasonable choice for concept editing. I used the paper's splits: 128 harmful prompts ([AdvBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15043), [MaliciousInstruct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06987), TDC2023) and 128 harmless ([Alpaca](https://github.com/tatsu-lab/stanford_alpaca)).

**Not any token position.** The chat template wraps your text, and everything after the instruction — the post-instruction region, 5 tokens for Qwen2.5 — is where the model has read the request and is about to answer. Means are taken there. With 5 positions and `L` layers you get `5 × L` candidates and have to pick one.

**Picking one.** Each candidate gets scored on 32 held-out harmful and 32 harmless prompts: *bypass* (ablate it — does refusal drop on harmful prompts), *induce* (add it to harmless prompts — does refusal rise, which it must, or it isn't really refusal), and *KL* (how far ablation drags the model on harmless text). Take the lowest bypass subject to `induce > 0`, KL under a ceiling, and `layer < 0.8L`, so you're cutting a concept and not just blocking the literal token "I".

**Erasing it.** Remove the component along `r̂`:

```
x' = x − r̂ r̂ᵀ x
```

`r̂ᵀx` is a scalar — how much of `r̂` is in `x`. Subtract it and `x'` has no component along `r̂` at all. One axis flattened, the other few thousand untouched. The paper shows this is equivalent to a permanent rank-one weight edit (that's abliteration). I used inference-time hooks instead, so I could vary the strength.

---

## What α actually is

The knob:

```
x' = x − α · r̂ r̂ᵀ x
```

`α = 0` is the untouched model, `α = 1` is the paper's full ablation. The obvious reading is "α is the fraction of the direction I remove". That reading is wrong, and getting it right is half the result.

I apply the ablation at **every layer**, and at every point where attention or the MLP writes back into the stream. So the scaling compounds. A component crossing `k` layers keeps about `(1−α)ᵏ` of itself, not `(1−α)`. At `α = 0.05` the direction is already ~80% gone by the layer it came from on 14B. By `α ≈ 0.25` it's gone everywhere.

So **α is a soft switch, not a dimmer.** Below some small value the direction survives; above it the direction is essentially erased and behaviour saturates. The sweep measures where that switch sits and what it costs. It does not trace a clean 0→100% erasure, and the small numbers below are a property of the intervention, not a claim that the model is touch-sensitive. One consequence: α is not comparable across depths. 14B has 48 layers to compound through, 7B has 28.

**Hooks.** A forward pre-hook rewrites a module's input, a forward hook rewrites its output. I ablate in three places per layer — block input, attention output, MLP output — so the direction can't be written back anywhere. A context manager always removes them, even on a crash. Sanity check: at `α = 0` the hooked model's logits match the clean model to the bit (`identity_err = 0.0`).

## What I ran

| Run | Models | α grid | Decoding | Eval prompts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pilot | 0.5B, 1.5B | 0→1.25, 11 pts | greedy, 1 sample | the 32 val prompts |
| Main | 0.5B | 0, 0.5, 1 | sampled, k=2 | 60 harmful / 60 XSTest / 60 Alpaca |
| Main | 7B | 0→1.25, step 0.125 | sampled, k=5 | 194 harmful / 100 XSTest / 60 Alpaca |
| Main | 14B | dense 0→0.3, +0.5, +1 | sampled, k=5 | 194 harmful / 100 XSTest / 60 Alpaca |

Harmful eval is HarmBench + AdvBench, disjoint from the extraction prompts. XSTest checks over-refusal — safe prompts that sound alarming. Alpaca is plain benign. At each α I logged CE loss on benign text, KL from the `α = 0` model, and multiple-choice accuracy on HellaSwag and TruthfulQA.

The pilot is in-sample: those 32 prompts are the ones that built the direction. I treat it as a smoke test only. It's dashed in the first plot because it's my only 1.5B data, and nothing rests on it.

**Where I deviated from the paper, and it matters:**

- **KL ceiling 1.0 instead of the paper's 0.1.** This let through directions the paper would have rejected. 7B landed on layer 17 at KL 0.71; the strict rule gives layer 15 at KL 0.063. I think this is why 7B shows more collateral damage than the paper reports.
- **0.5B has no valid direction.** Nothing passed the filters, so my code fell back to best-bypass. That direction has `induce = −0.95` — adding it doesn't produce refusal, so by the paper's own test it isn't a refusal direction. It still bypasses when ablated, which is interesting, but it is not a replication and I don't read it as one.
- **No LlamaGuard safety score.** I measure whether the model *refuses*, not whether the output is *harmful*. Biggest gap in the whole thing.
- Minor: refusal tokens auto-discovered (7B gave "I, Using, It" — "Using" is junk), no refusal-score filtering of prompts, sampled rather than greedy.

I didn't attempt the weight-orthogonalisation jailbreak, the adversarial-suffix analysis, or the base-model checks.

## Does the replication hold?

At `α = 1`, refusal goes to zero.

| Model | refusal at α=0 | at α=1 |
|---|---|---|
| Qwen2.5-7B | 0.816 | 0.000 |
| Qwen2.5-14B | 0.884 | 0.000 |
| Qwen2.5-0.5B | 0.458 | 0.000 |

Read the 0.5B row with the caveat above — that direction failed the paper's own validity test, so it belongs in the table as a curiosity, not as a third replication. The result is 7B and 14B.

Capability survives, with one exception that matches the paper. HellaSwag doesn't move (0.615→0.625 on 7B, 0.700→0.680 on 14B, inside the error bars). TruthfulQA drops hard (0.470→0.326 on 7B, 0.462→0.364 on 14B). Arditi report the same, and their explanation holds: TruthfulQA is full of conspiracy and misinformation items sitting right next to refusal territory, so a model that lost its guardrail answers them differently. That's the intervention working, not generic damage. XSTest over-refusal was already near zero on Qwen2.5, so there was no room for it to improve.

Worth saying plainly: 200 HellaSwag and 132 TruthfulQA items on one seed is a thin capability check. It's enough to say "nothing obviously broke", not enough to say "capability is preserved".

## Where does refusal break?

7B drops from 0.816 to 0.033 at `α = 0.125`, the first step of my grid — too coarse to see the edge. So on 14B I put ten points below 0.3:

| α | 0 | 0.06 | 0.09 | 0.12 | 0.15 | 0.18 | 0.21 | 0.24 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| refusal | 0.884 | 0.365 | 0.177 | 0.077 | 0.033 | 0.020 | 0.006 | 0.000 |

![Refusal rate against α across the model family](/research/fig1_alpha_curves.png)

Half the refusal is gone by `α ≈ 0.05`, all of it by `0.24`, and from `0.24` to `1.25` the curve is flat.

The low threshold is mostly the compounding, as set up above. The model fact is the saturation: past `α ≈ 0.25` more ablation does nothing. My `α = 0.25` and the paper's `α = 1` give you the same model. So the answer to "is there a cheap setting" is yes, and the guardrail does not give you a dial — it snaps.

---

## Does pushing harder cost more?

No, and this is the one I didn't expect. The worst point isn't `α = 1`. It sits in the middle, right where refusal is dying.

| Model | CE at α=0 | peak CE | CE at end of sweep |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7B | 3.32 | 4.83 (α=0.125 & 0.25) | 3.62 |
| 14B | 3.09 | 3.33 (α=0.09) | 3.16 |

![Refusal against language-model damage: CE peaks mid-sweep](/research/fig2_refusal_vs_damage.png)

On 7B, KL from the clean model peaks at 1.61 and falls to 0.48 by the end. Full ablation is *gentler* than partial ablation.

I don't have a proven explanation, so this is a guess: partial ablation leaves the model somewhere it never saw in training. The refusal signal is weak but still present, something downstream reads it, and then gets contradicted. Full ablation removes the signal and that's a cleaner state to be in. If that's right it's an instance of something more general — a half-broken feature can hurt a model more than a removed one. Checking it means looking at where the extra loss lands token by token, and I didn't do that.

Caveat, and it's a real one: the 7B swing is inflated by my loose KL filter, which picked a direction the paper would have thrown out. 14B, with a cleaner direction, shows the same shape much smaller (3.09→3.33→3.16). The shape is real. Don't quote the 7B magnitude on its own.

**Practical reading:** there is no sweet spot where you get partial removal cheaply. The middle is the expensive place to stand.

## How much do I trust the refusal metric?

Refusal here is keyword matching — does the completion contain "I'm sorry", "I cannot", and a handful of others. It's standard, and the paper flags it as limited. In a sweep it becomes a real problem, because the interesting behaviour is in the middle. Widen the list to catch "I will not", "I must decline" and similar hedges, and 14B's curve moves a lot in exactly the transition region:

| α | 0.06 | 0.09 | 0.12 | 0.15 | 0.18 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| list I used | 0.365 | 0.177 | 0.077 | 0.033 | 0.020 |
| broader list | 0.619 | 0.444 | 0.284 | 0.145 | 0.057 |

![Refusal metric sensitivity to the phrase list](/research/fig4_metric_sensitivity.png)

At `α = 0.06` that's the difference between "refuses a third of the time" and "two thirds". Reading completions, refusal doesn't switch off. It degrades through stages: flat refusal, then a hedged refusal that redirects, then a reply that answers while arguing against the premise, then plain compliance. Keyword matching collapses the middle stages into "not a refusal" and makes the edge look sharper than it is.

And the metric can't see harm at all. A completion with no refusal phrase counts as compliance whether it's genuinely harmful or a polite deflection. So I can say refusal *behaviour* disappears. I can't put a number on how much the model actually complies.

---

## Back to the questions

**Is the mechanism reliable?** No. On 7B and 14B, one direction from 128 prompt pairs, no gradients, no harmful completions needed, and refusal is gone. It also saturates early, so an attacker doesn't even need to do it properly.

**Can you flip the decision both ways?** Yes — ablate to remove refusal, add the same vector to induce it on harmless prompts. That's the induce score in the selection step, and it's why a direction that fails it (my 0.5B) isn't really a refusal direction.

**Does it generalise across models?** Across 7B and 14B of one family, yes, and Arditi show it across 13 models and four families.

**Are open-weight models in trouble?** For this guardrail, yes. The cost of removing it is a forward pass and some hooks.

**At what stage should refusal be introduced?** This is the question I actually care about; and the geometry gives a hint. A single direction sitting at the post-instruction tokens suggests refusal works like this:

```
query → start complying → "wait, is this a bad one?" → emit refusal
```

A check bolted onto the output.

What I'd want to see instead:

```
query → understand the situation → understand the consequences → respond
```

Refusal as a consequence of understanding, not a filter slapped on later.
