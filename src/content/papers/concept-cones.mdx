## What difference-in-means was missing

Difference-in-means reports where two clouds of activations differ on average. It has no
notion of what makes a direction good, so a sweep over layers and positions has to pick
whichever candidate happens to score best after the fact.

RDO defines what good means and optimises for it directly, with three requirements applied
at once rather than checked afterwards:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \lambda_{\text{abl}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{ablation}}
+ \lambda_{\text{add}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{addition}}
+ \lambda_{\text{ret}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{retain}}
$$

with weights $1.0$, $0.2$ and $1.0$, renormalising the direction at each step. The retain
term is the one difference-in-means has no analogue for: it explicitly penalises changing
behaviour on harmless prompts.

That shows up in the damage. Ablating the optimised direction costs about 40% less
TruthfulQA error than ablating the difference-in-means one — on Qwen2.5-14B, 67.9 against
63.3, with 70.8 for the untouched model. Which is worth noticing in the other direction
too: some of what we call the cost of abliteration is really a measure of how badly
difference-in-means locates the target, not of how entangled refusal is.

## From a direction to a cone

The larger move is optimising a set. Instead of one vector, RCO trains an orthonormal basis
$B = [b_1, \dots, b_N]$ and studies the region

$$
\Big\{ \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i b_i \;\Big|\; \lambda_i \ge 0 \Big\}
$$

requiring that vectors sampled from inside it all mediate refusal. The sampling detail is
easy to skip but matters: they sample uniformly over the non-negative part of the unit
sphere. Sampling convex combinations of the basis vectors instead would concentrate mass
near the corners and never test the interior.

They find such cones up to five dimensions, in every model — Qwen2.5 at four sizes, Gemma 2
at two, and Llama-3 8B. The higher-dimensional cones show up in the wider models, where the
residual stream is 5120 rather than 1536. Scale gives the model more room to spread the
behaviour out, which means this gets worse as models grow, not better.

The consequence is what I keep coming back to. Removing one line from a solid region leaves
the region. Any defence that deletes a rank-one subspace is patching one hole in a wall.

## Orthogonality is not independence

Find two refusal directions, observe that they are orthogonal, and the natural conclusion is
that they are separate mechanisms. The paper shows that conclusion does not follow. They
take a direction strictly orthogonal to the difference-in-means direction, ablate it, and
watch the other one. In the later layers its expression in the activations drops anyway.

The reason is straightforward once said out loud. Orthogonality is a statement about two
vectors at a single point in the network. Between that point and the output sit attention
and MLP layers, which are not linear. A right angle at layer 12 says nothing about what a
non-linear function does at layer 20.

So they define the stronger property. Two directions are **representationally independent**
if ablating one leaves the cosine similarity of the activations with the other unchanged at
every layer. It is enforced with an extra loss over the first 90% of layers — nearer the
output the constraint becomes too tight to satisfy.

Enforcing it costs attack strength, but at least five independent directions survive in
Gemma-2-2B, each still well above a random vector. And they are reachable from the input: a
GCG suffix optimised against one of them jailbreaks the model 36% of the time. That number
is what makes representational independence more than a latent curiosity — the model is
genuinely using these directions, and token-level attacks can get at them.

## Why this matters beyond refusal

Take the two results together and a lot of defensive work looks shaky.

If a concept occupies a cone rather than a line, removing a rank-one subspace is not
removing the concept. And if orthogonality does not certify independence, then every method
treating orthogonality as evidence of having isolated a concept is proving less than it
claims.

That second point lands on [Safety Subspaces](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14185), which
measures subspace overlap and concludes safety is not linearly separable from general
capability. Its metric treats orthogonality as what separation would look like. By this
paper's argument, even a perfectly orthogonal result would not have established
independence — so the conclusion survives and gets stronger: subspace defences fail on two
counts rather than one.

## Where I think it is weak

Every direction is optimised against the same set of target responses, so the cones found
are a lower bound on the mess. Other regions of activation space could mediate refusal in
ways this objective never looks for.

And the intervention is still orthogonal projection, in a space that is still
[anisotropic](https://aclanthology.org/D19-1006/). The paper sharpens *what* you cut out
without touching the geometry you cut in. I would want the whole analysis redone under a
whitened metric before believing the cone dimensions are the real ones rather than an
artefact of measuring angles in a distorted space.
