## Finding the direction

Run harmful and harmless instructions through the model. At layer $\ell$ and token
position $i$, average the residual stream activations over each set and subtract:

$$
r = \mu_{\text{harmful}} - \nu_{\text{harmless}}
$$

Normalise to unit length and you have a candidate $\hat{r}$. That is the whole extraction
method — difference-in-means, no training.

Doing this for every layer and every post-instruction position gives a large pool of
candidates. One is chosen using three scores: does ablating it stop refusal, does adding
it cause refusal, and does ablating it leave harmless prompts alone (measured as KL
divergence). Candidates from the last 20% of layers are excluded, which matters more than
it looks — without that cutoff the search would happily return a direction that points at
refusal *tokens* in the output vocabulary rather than the upstream representation deciding
to emit them.

It works because of *where* it looks. The tokens after the instruction are aggregation
points: attention has already pulled the whole prompt into them, so a single position
summarises the request, including whether it looked harmful.

One detail worth carrying around: where a direction is **extracted** and where it is
**applied** need not be the same place, and here they are not. The vector is taken from
one layer and one token; activation addition is applied at that layer across all tokens;
full ablation is applied across all layers and all tokens. This matters whenever two
directions are compared — the comparison is unfair if one is applied more broadly than the
other.

The direction is also per-model. Llama's refusal direction does nothing in Gemma. There is
no portable refusal vector.

## Three things you can do with it

**Ablate it.** At every layer and position, subtract the component along the direction:

$$
x' = x - \hat{r}\hat{r}^\top x
$$

The model now answers harmful requests, and stays coherent doing it.

**Add it.** Add $r$ into activations at one layer and a harmless prompt gets refused. This
is the half that makes the claim causal: the direction does not merely correlate with
refusal, it drives it in both directions.

**Put it in the weights.** Every matrix $W_{\text{out}}$ that writes into the residual
stream — embeddings, attention output, MLP output — is orthogonalised against the
direction:

$$
W'_{\text{out}} = W_{\text{out}} - \hat{r}\hat{r}^\top W_{\text{out}}
$$

Appendix E proves this is exactly equivalent to ablating at inference. Geometrically you
take the whole activation space and flatten it along one axis, so the model becomes
physically unable to write into the refusal subspace. What comes out is a normal set of
weights you can save and share.

The cost profile is the point. Fine-tuning a model to remove safety needs harmful
instructions *and* harmful answers, plus an optimiser. This needs 128 harmful instructions,
one forward pass each, and a matrix subtraction. On the HarmBench test set it reaches
79–84% attack success on the Qwen models, against 0–42% for human-written jailbreak
templates.

## What adversarial suffixes were doing all along

Section 5 measures how strongly activations point along $\hat{r}$ under three conditions:
a plain harmful prompt, a harmful prompt with a random suffix, and a harmful prompt with an
adversarial suffix. Under the adversarial suffix the alignment collapses to the level of an
ordinary harmless prompt.

Then they find the mechanism. Direct feature attribution identifies the attention heads
that write most strongly toward refusal. Normally those heads attend to the harmful words
in the instruction. The adversarial suffix pulls their attention onto itself, so the heads
stop seeing the dangerous content, never send the signal, and generation proceeds as if the
request were ordinary.

So GCG is not finding an exotic new failure. It is suppressing this direction from outside
the model, where the weight edit removes the ability to write it at all.

## Refusal direction is a functional name

This is the caveat I think gets dropped most often when the paper is cited.

What was shown is that among the layers and positions examined, this direction most
effectively controls refusal **behaviour**. It was not shown that the direction is a clean
semantic axis of harm versus not-harm. The authors are explicit about it: the vector might
encode harm, or danger, or nothing with a simple interpretation. "Refusal direction" names
what it does, not what it means.

The honest phrasing of the result is that a one-dimensional component was found which is
functionally necessary for realising refusal behaviour in these chat models — which is a
narrower and more defensible claim than the one the title suggests.

## Where I think it is weak

**Negative activation addition is not symmetric**, and this is the part I find most
underrated. A harmful prompt already sits high on the refusal component, so subtracting
moves it toward the non-refusal region — roughly where it needs to go. A harmless prompt is
already low there, and the same offset drags it far outside its natural distribution. That
asymmetry is why removing the direction can bypass refusal and still break benign behaviour
and raise perplexity. The paper observes the effect itself.

**The extraction method is a baseline.** Difference-in-means is close to the simplest thing
that could have worked, and the paper is honest that the goal was demonstrating fragility
rather than finding the best direction. [Concept Cones](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17420)
replaces the sweep with gradient descent and finds not just a better direction but a whole
cone of them.

**The projection assumes a space it does not have.** Orthogonal projection assumes cutting
along one axis leaves everything else alone. Representation space in a transformer is
[anisotropic](https://aclanthology.org/D19-1006/): activations occupy a narrow cone and a
few dimensions carry variance far out of proportion to the rest. An orthogonal step in a
space shaped like that is a large step along whatever high-variance axis is nearby. The
measured cost is real — Concept Cones found ablation dropping Qwen2.5-14B from 70.8 to 63.3
on TruthfulQA, so something other than refusal sits on that axis.

Underneath all of it sits the linear representation hypothesis, which carries the paper and
is not tested in it. And everything here is open models up to 72B.

This is the thread running through most of the rest of the list.
[LEACE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03819) whitens the space before projecting for exactly
this reason. The [causal inner product](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03658) tries to define
the right ruler formally. And [Safety Subspaces](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14185) argues
the target may not be separable at all.
