## The method

Take frozen representations $X$ and a protected attribute $Z$.

1. Train a linear classifier to predict $Z$ from $X$.
2. Project $X$ onto that classifier's nullspace. The classifier is now useless, because
   the direction it relied on is gone.
3. Repeat. Train a fresh classifier on the projected representations, and it will find
   another direction. Project that out too.

After enough rounds, no linear classifier can recover the attribute. That is linear
guardedness, and INLP was the first practical route to it.

## What it costs

Every iteration deletes a direction from the representation, permanently. Around 20
iterations are typically needed, and the cumulative cost is roughly 360 destroyed
dimensions.

That the loop needs 20 rounds at all is informative. If the attribute lived in one
direction, one projection would finish the job. It does not, because the information is
distributed and correlated across many directions, so removing one leaves enough behind for
a fresh classifier to find another.

The same thing shows up in [LEACE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03819) as rediscovery across
layers, and in [Concept Cones](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17420) as a cone rather than a
line. Three different setups, one recurring observation: a concept is never in only one
place.

## Why it matters for abliteration

INLP and abliteration are the same manoeuvre at different scales. Both find a direction
that carries a concept and remove it by orthogonal projection.

INLP's 360-versus-17 comparison against LEACE is the clearest available price tag on
projecting orthogonally in a space that is not round. LEACE gets the identical guarantee by
whitening first, projecting in the corrected space, and unwhitening — and keeps 343 more
dimensions of the representation.

Nobody has run that comparison for refusal directions. It is the experiment I most want to
see.
