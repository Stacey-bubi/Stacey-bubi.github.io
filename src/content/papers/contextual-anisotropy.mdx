## What anisotropy means, concretely

An **isotropic** space would have the embedding cloud shaped like a ball: variance roughly
equal in every direction, no preferred axis. In that world cosine similarity behaves. Two
unrelated words point in unrelated directions and score near zero, so a score of 0.7 means
something.

Real transformer representations are not shaped like that. They occupy a narrow
high-dimensional cone. Every vector shares a large common component, so every pair of
vectors already looks similar. Ethayarajh's measurement is the memorable part: in the upper
layers, two random words with nothing to do with each other score around 0.9.

The similarity you measure is mostly the shape of the space, not the relationship between
the words.

## Why the corrected measures matter

If random pairs already score 0.9, then reporting that two occurrences of the same word
score 0.95 tells you almost nothing. The paper's fix is to subtract the baseline: measure
the expected similarity of random word pairs *in that same layer*, and report everything
relative to it.

This is the same move LEACE makes geometrically. Both are saying: correct for the
distribution before you measure distances in it.

## Why I care about a 2019 paper

Every intervention method on this list does arithmetic in this space.

Abliteration finds a direction by subtracting two means and removes it by orthogonal
projection(it works empirically). [Safety Subspaces](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14185) compares subspaces with
principal angles. [Concept Cones](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17420) checks whether two
directions are orthogonal. All three are Euclidean operations, and all three assume the
coordinate system is meaningful.

In an anisotropic space, that assumption is doing unexamined work. A step that is small in
coordinates can be large in the distribution, and two directions that look orthogonal can
be highly correlated in terms of what the model actually does with them.

There are some serious responses in the literature.
[LEACE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03819) whitens the space, projects in the whitened
coordinates, and unwhitens, so the projection happens where orthogonality means what you
want. [The causal inner product](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03658) argues for a specific
non-Euclidean inner product, built from the inverse covariance of the vocabulary vectors,
under which causally independent concepts really are orthogonal.
