## What MSO measures

The paper needs a way to ask whether two sets of weight updates point in the same
directions. It takes the SVD of each, keeps the top principal components as orthonormal
bases $Q_1$ and $Q_2$, and computes

$$
\text{MSO} = \frac{\lVert Q_1^\top Q_2 \rVert_F^2}{\min(k_1, k_2)}
$$

the average squared cosine between the two sets of principal directions. Zero means
orthogonal subspaces. Anything meaningfully above the random baseline means the two updates
move the model along shared directions.

They compute this between three kinds of update: $\Delta^{\text{Harmful}}$ (fine-tuning on
harmful data), $\Delta^{\text{Useful}}$ (fine-tuning on ordinary capability data), and
$\Delta^{\text{Alignment}}$ (safety training).

## The result that should worry people

The overlap between harmful and useful updates is **higher** than between harmful and
alignment updates.

Read that again in terms of what it implies. If you wanted a defence that watches for weight
updates pointing in "harmful" directions, the updates it would flag look more like ordinary
capability learning than like the safety training they are supposed to be undoing. There is
no direction to watch that does not also catch the model getting better at its job.

The projection experiments confirm it behaviourally. Cut along the directions carrying
harmful behaviour and utility comes away with it, every time.

## Why fine-tuning on harmless data breaks alignment

This gives the mechanism for a result that had been reported but never really explained. It
is not that harmless datasets secretly contain harmful content, and it is not adversarial.
It is that the directions ordinary fine-tuning moves are the same directions safety
occupies. Damage to alignment is a side effect of learning anything at all.

That also makes this the strongest available argument against a whole family of defences.
SafeLoRA and its relatives assume there is a safety subspace to protect or restore. If the
subspace is not distinct, the method is protecting a region that also contains the model's
usefulness.

## The tension with Concept Cones, and why it strengthens the case

Worth being precise about, because it looks at first like a problem for this paper.

MSO treats orthogonality as the standard for separability — low overlap would have meant
separable subspaces. But [Concept Cones](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17420) shows
orthogonality does not imply independence under intervention: ablate one direction and an
exactly orthogonal one still weakens downstream, because non-linear layers sit in between.

So this paper's criterion is, if anything, too generous. It looked for orthogonality, failed
to find it, and concluded the subspaces are entangled. Had it found orthogonality, that
still would not have established independence.

Both failure modes are real and independent of each other. Subspace-based defences have to
clear two bars, and this line of work says they clear neither.

## Where I think it is weak

Only the **linear** case is tested. Non-linear disentanglement, activation patching and
causal independence are all left open. The paper shows safety is not *linearly* distinct,
which is not the same as showing it is not distinct.

MSO is also a geometric overlap measure rather than a causal one. High overlap is strong
evidence two subspaces cannot be cut apart linearly, but it does not by itself establish
what is causally responsible for behaviour.

And it is a fine-tuning case study. How far the conclusion generalises beyond that setting
is not settled here.
