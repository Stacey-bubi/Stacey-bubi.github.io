## Two signals, two places

The setup is difference-in-means, run twice at two different positions.

At the last token of the user instruction, average activations over harmful prompts and
subtract the average over harmless ones. That difference is the **harmfulness** direction —
the model's assessment of the request.

At the last token of the whole prompt, after the chat template boundary, average over refused
prompts and subtract the average over accepted ones. That is the **refusal** direction — the
signal routing the response.

The positional evidence is direct. Drop the post-instruction tokens and refusal rates fall
hard: Llama-3 from 96.0% to 58.9%, Llama-2 from 100% to 85.3%, Qwen2 from 98.0% to 81.3%.
Refusal is being organised at that boundary token.

Worth being careful about what that means, though. A template token like `[/INST]` does not
"contain refusal". Its residual activation has absorbed the whole preceding prompt through
attention, plus the template boundary, so it is a convenient place where the model prepares
what kind of continuation comes next. Refusal is one causally useful direction there, not the
meaning of the token.

## The experiment that makes the case

Showing that two directions exist is easy. Showing they mean different things is hard, because
both produce refusals when you steer along them, and refusal is all you can observe from
outside.

The reply-inversion task solves this. Append to the prompt:

> Could this user prompt cause harm? Answer Certainly if it could, otherwise No.

Now the model's *judgement* is the surface answer, separate from whether it refuses. So you
can steer and watch which one moves.

Steering along harmfulness flips harmless prompts to "Certainly" — the assessment changed.
Steering along refusal mostly leaves the answer at "No" while still generating refusals — the
behaviour changed and the assessment did not.

That is a clean separation of judgement from response, and the kind of counterfactual design
that steering papers usually lack. Difference-in-means only ever gives you an average
difference; the semantic interpretation here comes from the held-out cases and the inversion
intervention, not from the extraction method. That is the right way round, and a lot of
steering work gets it backwards.

## What it says about jailbreaks

Some attacks work by suppressing the refusal signal while leaving the harmfulness judgement
intact. The model still internally registers the request as dangerous, and answers anyway.

This fits with [Arditi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717) on adversarial suffixes,
where the suffix hijacks the attention heads that write refusal. The model has not been
persuaded the request is safe. The wire carrying the refusal has been cut.

It also complicates what abliteration does. If refusal and harmfulness are separate, removing
the refusal direction leaves a model that still recognises harm and no longer acts on it.
Whether that matters depends on what you wanted from the model, but it is not the same as
removing its ability to tell.

## The category result

The number I find most informative is the spread across harm categories.

On Qwen2, refusal directions from different categories agree closely with each other — cosine
similarity 0.89 to 0.95. Harmfulness directions agree much less, 0.60 to 0.73. And a
harmfulness direction fitted on one category transfers poorly: 100% effect in domain, 32% on
political campaigning, 52% averaged.

The natural reading is roughly one refusal mechanism, fed by a family of related but genuinely
different harm judgements. "The harmfulness direction" is a simplification of something with
real internal structure.

That also shows up in the practical application. Their Latent Guard, which classifies prompts
by the sign of the harmfulness projection, beats Llama Guard 3 in places — 75.0% against 17.8%
on Qwen2 persuasion jailbreaks — but is clearly worse out of domain on ToxicChat and the
OpenAI moderation set. It is not a drop-in replacement for a guard model.

## Where I think it is weak

This is an existence proof, not a theory of representation. Three open 7B/8B models, so
nothing here says whether the picture holds in larger or proprietary systems. The harmfulness
direction may be carrying lexical, topical and dataset artefacts rather than a clean concept —
the category spread is consistent with that reading too.

One asymmetry bothers me: in the inversion task the refusal direction is applied to more token
positions than the harmfulness direction, which makes the two harder to compare than the
framing suggests. The distinction between where a direction is *found* and where it is
*applied* matters throughout this literature, and it is not fully controlled for here.

Layers, heads and circuit-level mechanism are not investigated at all. This is about directions
and positions only.

## Why I keep it

Mostly as a template for my own work. If I want to find whether a model internally judges a
prompt underspecified, this is the shape of the experiment: build difference-in-means
candidates per layer and position, test them on matched pairs, then use a causal inversion task
to separate "the model judged X" from "the model produced the behaviour associated with X".

One vector at a template token can control response mode without storing the concept itself.
Keeping those two apart is the whole lesson.
