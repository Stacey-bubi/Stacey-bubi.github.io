## Two signals, two places

The setup is difference-in-means, run twice at two different positions.

At $t_{\text{inst}}$, the last token of the user instruction, average activations over
harmful prompts and subtract the average over harmless ones. That difference is the
**harmfulness** direction — the model's assessment of the request.

At $t_{\text{post-inst}}$, the last token of the whole prompt after the chat template
boundary, average over refused prompts and subtract the average over accepted ones. That
is the **refusal** direction — the signal that routes the response.

The positional evidence is direct. Drop the post-instruction tokens and refusal rates fall
hard: Llama-3 goes from 96.0% to 58.9%. Refusal is being organised at that boundary token.

## The experiment that makes the case

Showing that two directions exist is easy. Showing they mean different things is the hard
part, because both produce refusals when you steer along them, and refusal is all you can
observe from outside.

The reply-inversion task solves this. Append to the prompt:

> Could this user prompt cause harm? Answer Certainly if it could, otherwise No.

Now the model's *judgement* is the surface answer, separate from whether it refuses. So you
can steer and watch which one moves.

Steering along harmfulness flips harmless prompts to "Certainly" — the model's assessment
changed. Steering along refusal mostly leaves the answer at "No" while still generating
refusals — the behaviour changed and the assessment did not.

That is a clean separation of judgement from response, and it is the kind of counterfactual
design that steering papers usually lack.

## What it says about jailbreaks

Some attacks work by suppressing the refusal signal while leaving the harmfulness judgement
intact. The model still internally registers the request as dangerous, and answers anyway.

This fits with [Arditi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717) on adversarial suffixes,
where the suffix hijacks the attention heads that write refusal. The model has not been
persuaded the request is safe. The wire carrying the refusal has been cut.

It also complicates what abliteration is doing. If refusal and harmfulness are separate,
removing the refusal direction leaves a model that still recognises harm and no longer acts
on it. Whether that matters depends on what you wanted from the model, but it is not the
same thing as removing its ability to tell.

## The category result

The number I find most informative is the spread across harm categories.

Refusal directions from different categories agree closely with each other, cosine
similarity 0.89–0.95 on Qwen2. Harmfulness directions agree much less, 0.60–0.73. And a
harmfulness direction fitted on one category transfers poorly: 100% effect in domain, 32%
on political campaigning, 52% averaged.

The natural reading is that there is roughly one refusal mechanism, fed by a family of
related but genuinely different harm judgements. "The harmfulness direction" is a
simplification of something with real internal structure — which is also why Latent Guard
falls apart out of domain.
