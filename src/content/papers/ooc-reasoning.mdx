## The claim

Out-of-context reasoning is the observation that a fine-tuned model sometimes generalises
much further than the fine-tuning data should support. It has been treated as evidence of
something structured going on inside.

This paper offers a duller explanation for many of the reported cases. LoRA fine-tuning
ends up adding what amounts to a constant steering vector to the activations, pushing the
model toward some general concept. That single push helps on the fine-tuning task and on
every other task related to that concept, which is what the surprising generalisation
consists of.

Two pieces of evidence support it. Steering vectors trained from scratch, without any
fine-tuning, reproduce the behaviour. And the same explanation holds for backdoors, where
adding the vector unconditionally is enough — even though the behaviour looks like it must
depend on a trigger.

## Why the backdoor case is the interesting one

A backdoor is the textbook example of conditional computation: behave normally, unless the
trigger appears.

If an unconditional constant vector reproduces that, the conditionality is not stored as a
rule the model checks. It emerges from how a constant push interacts with context — the
same nudge only changes the output when the surrounding context makes that output
plausible.

That is a genuinely different mental model of what fine-tuning does, and a more tractable
one.

## What it means for the rest of this list

If fine-tuning mostly moves models along a small number of directions, then two things
that are usually discussed separately are closer than they look.
[Abliteration](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717) edits activations or weights along a
direction. Fine-tuning attacks retrain the model. If the retraining is largely equivalent
to adding a direction, the two attack families overlap heavily, and a defence against one
has a chance against the other.

It also gives a mechanism for the result in
[Safety Subspaces](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14185). If fine-tuning is a push along a few
directions, and safety occupies directions that overlap with general capability, then
harmless fine-tuning breaking alignment is exactly what you would predict.

The general lesson is the one I want to keep: check whether a constant offset explains the
behaviour before concluding the model learned something clever.
