## Why abliteration needs short refusals

[Abliteration](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717) finds its direction by averaging
activations on harmful prompts, averaging on harmless ones, and subtracting. That works
best when the two sets differ in a consistent, concentrated way.

Templated refusals give it exactly that. Every refusal starts with much the same handful of
tokens, so the difference between the harmful and harmless activation means is sharp,
low-dimensional and easy to locate.

The attack's requirement is not that refusal exists. It is that refusal is *concentrated*.

## The defence

Retrain the model so a refusal has three parts:

1. A neutral explanation of the topic.
2. The explicit refusal.
3. An ethical justification of why the request is unacceptable.

Now the safety-relevant content is distributed across many tokens and many dimensions.
Difference-in-means still returns *a* direction, but no single direction carries the
behaviour, so removing one does not remove refusal.

The measured drop under attack goes from 70–80 points to under 10.

## Why the coherence constraint matters

It would be easy to fake these results. Pick the direction that most reduces refusals and
you can usually find one that reduces refusals to zero by destroying the model — it stops
refusing because it has stopped making sense.

The paper filters directions by requiring coherence at or above 70%, so a direction only
counts if the model is still producing usable text after the edit. Any evaluation of an
abliteration defence without a constraint like this should be treated with suspicion.

## Where I would push on it

The evaluation attacks the defended model with single-direction ablation, which is the
attack the defence was designed against.

The interesting question is what happens under
[Refusal Cone Optimisation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17420), fitted directly against the
extended-refusal model. If the safety signal is genuinely spread across many dimensions,
then a cone-based attack should need a higher-dimensional cone to remove it — which would
be a much stronger result than the one reported, and a quantitative one. If instead a
five-dimensional cone still strips it out, the defence is buying less than it appears to.

Either answer would be worth knowing, and the experiment is not expensive.
