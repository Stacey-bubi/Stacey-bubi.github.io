## What MSO measures

The paper needs a way to ask whether two sets of weight updates point in the same
directions. It takes the SVD of each, keeps the top principal components as orthonormal
bases $Q_1$ and $Q_2$, and computes

$$
\text{MSO} = \frac{\lVert Q_1^\top Q_2 \rVert_F^2}{\min(k_1, k_2)}
$$

which is the average squared cosine between the two sets of principal directions. Zero
means orthogonal subspaces. Anything meaningfully above the random baseline means the two
updates are moving the model along shared directions.

They compute this between three kinds of update: $\Delta^{\text{Harmful}}$ (fine-tuning on
harmful data), $\Delta^{\text{Useful}}$ (fine-tuning on ordinary capability data), and
$\Delta^{\text{Alignment}}$ (safety training).

## The result that should worry people

The overlap between harmful and useful updates is **higher** than between harmful and
alignment updates.

Read that again in terms of what it implies. If you wanted to build a defence that watches
for weight updates pointing in "harmful" directions, the updates it would flag look more
like ordinary capability learning than like the safety training they are supposed to be
undoing. There is no direction to watch that does not also catch the model getting better
at its job.

The projection experiments confirm it behaviourally. Cut along the directions that carry
harmful behaviour and utility comes away with it, every time.

## Why fine-tuning on harmless data breaks alignment

This gives the mechanism for a result that had been reported but not really explained. It
is not that harmless datasets secretly contain harmful content, and it is not adversarial.
It is that the directions ordinary fine-tuning moves are the same directions safety
occupies. Damage to alignment is a side effect of learning anything at all.

## The tension with Concept Cones, and why it strengthens the case

Worth being precise about, because it looks at first like a problem for this paper.

MSO treats orthogonality as the standard for separability. Low overlap would have meant
separable subspaces. But [Concept Cones](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17420) shows
orthogonality does not imply independence under intervention — ablate one direction and an
exactly orthogonal one still weakens downstream, because non-linear layers sit in between.

So this paper's criterion is, if anything, too generous. It looked for orthogonality,
failed to find it, and concluded the subspaces are entangled. Had it found orthogonality,
that still would not have established independence.

Both failure modes are real and they are independent of each other. Subspace-based
defences have to clear two bars, and this line of work says they clear neither.
