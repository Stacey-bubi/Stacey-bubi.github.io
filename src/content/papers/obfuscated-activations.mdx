## The threat model

Latent-space defenses rest on a reasonable premise. Even if you cannot filter every harmful
output, harmful behaviour must pass through the model's internal states, so watch those
instead. Train a probe on activations, or use a sparse autoencoder, or an OOD detector, and
catch the behaviour before it surfaces.

This paper asks whether the internal state is a reliable place to watch, and finds it is not.
Activations are malleable: the same harmful output can be produced from many different
internal configurations, and an attacker can choose one the monitor was never trained to flag.

## Four ways in

The attacks are graded by how much access the attacker has, which is what makes the result hard
to dismiss.

**Embedding suffix attacks** put a universal suffix in embedding space, optimised jointly for
the harmful behaviour and for evading the monitor. White-box. **Hard prompt attacks** use real
tokens, optimised per input with a gradient-free method, needing only output access.
**Fine-tuning with an obfuscation loss** applies when the attacker controls training, making
evasion an explicit objective. And **data poisoning** achieves obfuscation by choosing trigger
complexity and poison rate carefully, with nobody optimising for evasion at all.

That last one is the most uncomfortable. Evasion does not require an adversary who understands
the defence. It can be a side effect of how the poisoned data was constructed.

## The numbers that matter

Against harmfulness probes on Llama-3-8B-Instruct, obfuscation cuts recall from **100% to 0%**
while keeping roughly a **90% jailbreak rate**. The monitor does not degrade — it stops working
while the attack keeps working.

Generation-based probes, which watch activations during generation rather than on the prompt,
do better against ordinary jailbreaks: over 90% recall at a 1% false positive rate. Against
obfuscation they fail completely.

Adversarial training does not close the gap either. After more than 70 epochs of alternating
attack and defence, attackers were still bypassing the trained monitors, which suggests
obfuscated representations are abundant rather than rare.

## The sparse autoencoder result deserves more attention

SAE-based monitors fall to near-zero recall under embedding attacks while the model still
produces working SQL. Raw SAE latents are no more robust than plain probes.

This one is worth dwelling on, because SAEs are frequently described as recovering the features
the model actually uses — which should make an SAE-based monitor more principled than a probe
fitted to a label. Under attack they behave the same.

## Where the hope is

Obfuscation is not free. On SQL generation, obfuscating against more layers correlates
significantly with worse benchmark performance. Constraining your own internal states enough to
fool a monitor costs you the ability to do things that need those states.

That points somewhere different from better detectors. If evasion is expensive on hard tasks, a
defence might be built on requiring the model to do something that genuinely needs its full
representational capacity — making obfuscation and capability compete — rather than on
recognising what harm looks like from the inside.

## How I read it against the rest of the list

This is the strongest argument I know against treating latent monitoring as a security boundary.
A probe measures a correlation between activations and behaviour that held on the data it was
fitted on. An attacker optimising against the probe is optimising exactly that correlation away.

It also fits the pattern of the rest of this list from the defensive side.
[Concept Cones](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17420) shows many directions mediate a behaviour;
this shows an attacker can pick which of them to route through. If a behaviour can be produced
from many internal states, a monitor watching one region is watching a small part of the space.

The honest caveats: the strongest attacks need white-box access to embeddings, and hard prompt
attacks take considerable tuning. Black-box transferability is barely explored, which is what
would matter for API-served models. Some experiments use small samples. And the
obfuscation-versus-capability trade-off is demonstrated but not characterised, so there is no
way yet to say how much capability a given amount of evasion costs.
