## The method

Take frozen representations $X$ and a protected attribute $Z$.

1. Train a linear classifier to predict $Z$ from $X$.
2. Project $X$ onto that classifier's nullspace. The classifier is now useless, because the
   direction it relied on is gone.
3. Repeat. Train a fresh classifier on the projected representations and it will find another
   direction. Project that out too.

After enough rounds, no linear classifier can recover the attribute.

## What it costs

Every iteration deletes a direction from the representation, permanently. Around 20 iterations
are typically needed, and the cumulative cost is roughly **360 destroyed dimensions** — a figure
reported by the [LEACE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03819) authors when comparing against it.
LEACE achieves the same guarantee at a cost of **17**.

That gap is the clearest available price tag on projecting orthogonally in a space that is not
round. INLP deletes whole dimensions; LEACE whitens first, projects in the corrected space,
unwhitens, and deletes only the correlation.

The iteration count is informative in its own right. If the attribute lived in one direction,
one projection would finish the job. It does not, because the information is distributed and
correlated across many directions, so removing one leaves enough behind for a fresh classifier
to find another.

The same thing shows up in LEACE as rediscovery across layers, and in
[Concept Cones](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17420) as a cone rather than a line. Three different
setups, one recurring observation: a concept is never in only one place.

## Why it matters for abliteration

INLP and abliteration are the same manoeuvre at different scales. Both find a direction carrying
a concept and remove it by orthogonal projection, and both inherit the same problem — they take
unrelated information with the target because they do not correct for the shape of the space
first.

Historically this is the ancestor of the whole surgical-editing line, abliteration included.
Worth knowing where the family started, and that its main flaw was diagnosed and fixed three
years later.

Nobody has run the equivalent comparison for refusal directions. It is the experiment I most
want to see.

## Its limits

It removes far more of the representation than it needs to. It is iterative and adversarial in
flavour, so it is slower than a closed form and comes with no optimality guarantee about how
little it damages the representation.

And the guarantee covers linear classifiers only — the attribute may remain recoverable by a
non-linear probe. As with everything in this family, "a probe cannot read the attribute" is not
the same as fairness in behaviour, and that gap is still open.
