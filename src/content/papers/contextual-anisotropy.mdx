## What anisotropy means, concretely

An **isotropic** space would have the embedding cloud shaped like a ball: variance roughly
equal in every direction, no preferred axis. In that world cosine similarity behaves. Two
unrelated words point in unrelated directions and score near zero, so a score of 0.7 means
something.

Real transformer representations are not shaped like that. They occupy a narrow
high-dimensional cone. Every vector shares a large common component, so every pair already
looks similar. The measurement is the memorable part: in the upper layers, two random words
with nothing to do with each other score around 0.9.

The similarity you measure is mostly the shape of the space, not the relationship between
the words. The scale is broken before you use it.

## Why the corrected measures matter

If random pairs already score 0.9, then reporting that two occurrences of the same word
score 0.95 tells you almost nothing. The fix is to subtract the baseline: measure the
expected similarity of random word pairs *in that same layer*, and report everything
relative to it.

This is the same move LEACE makes geometrically. Both say: correct for the distribution
before you measure distances in it.

## Why I care about a 2019 paper

Every intervention method on this list does arithmetic in this space.

Abliteration finds a direction by subtracting two means and removes it by orthogonal
projection (it works empirically).
[Safety Subspaces](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14185) compares subspaces with principal
angles. [Concept Cones](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17420) checks whether two directions are
orthogonal. All three are Euclidean operations, and all three assume the coordinate system
is meaningful.

In an anisotropic space, that assumption is doing unexamined work. A step that is small in
coordinates can be large in the distribution, and two directions that look orthogonal can be
highly correlated in terms of what the model actually does with them.

There are some serious responses in the literature.
[LEACE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03819) whitens the space, projects in the whitened
coordinates, and unwhitens, so the projection happens where orthogonality means what you
want. [The causal inner product](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03658) argues for a specific
non-Euclidean inner product, built from the inverse covariance of the vocabulary vectors,
under which causally independent concepts really are orthogonal.

## Where the anisotropy comes from

Several causes, and they stack rather than compete.

Gao et al. (2019) traced part of it to training dynamics: cross-entropy over a Zipfian
vocabulary with weight tying pushes rare-token embeddings into a narrow region. Timkey and
van Schijndel (2021) showed that a handful of **rogue dimensions** with enormous variance
dominate every dot product.
[Superposition](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/toy_model/index.html) adds another:
features get packed in as nearly-orthogonal directions under a dimension budget.

Historically, Mu and Viswanath found the static-embedding version in 2018 (all-but-the-top).
This paper is where it was established for transformers.

## The limits of it

It is English only, on a small specialised corpus, with models that are old by current
standards — that the finding still holds in modern decoders comes from
[other work](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-eacl.58/), not from here.

It is also purely geometric. Nothing connects the measured anisotropy to performance on any
downstream task, so the practical size of the problem is still open — which is, awkwardly,
the thing I would most like to know.

And it describes anisotropy without explaining it. The mechanistic accounts came later and
from other people.
