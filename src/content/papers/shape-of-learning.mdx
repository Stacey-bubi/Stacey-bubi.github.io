## Two measurements

**Anisotropy** here is a spectral quantity. Centre the embedding matrix, take its singular
values, and compute

$$
\text{anisotropy}(X) = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sum_i \sigma_i^2}
$$

the fraction of total variance carried by the single largest direction. A value near zero
means variance is spread evenly, which is what an isotropic space looks like. A large value
means one direction dominates everything.

**Intrinsic dimension** asks how many dimensions the data actually occupies, regardless of
how many it is stored in. The estimator used works from how the volume of a sphere scales:
for each point, take the distances to its first and second nearest neighbours, and fit the
distribution of their ratio.

## Where the distortion lives

The result I use is the encoder/decoder split.

Encoders are fairly evenly anisotropic through their depth. Decoders are not: their
anisotropy profile is bell-shaped, peaking in the middle layers.

That is worth putting next to how refusal directions are extracted.
[Arditi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717) sweep layers and exclude the last 20% of
the network, so the chosen direction typically comes from the middle. According to this
paper, the middle is the most geometrically distorted part of a decoder.

So the standard abliteration recipe picks its direction from the region where orthogonal
projection is least justified. That does not make the attack wrong — it plainly works — but
it is a plausible source of the collateral damage, and it suggests the
[whitening correction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03819) would matter most exactly where
these methods operate.

## The training dynamics

Anisotropy in decoders grows through training and then levels off. The narrow cone is
something the model builds, not something it starts with.

Intrinsic dimension moves in two phases: up first, as the model spreads information into
more dimensions, then down, as it compresses into more compact representations. The final
model is not the most dimensionally spread version of itself — it passed through that on
the way.
